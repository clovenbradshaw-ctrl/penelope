The Waxen Tablet

There is an old image of memory as a waxen tablet: soft when new, capable of receiving “true thoughts and clear impressions,” hardening with use until it can no longer be written on. We have kept the image for twenty-four hundred years because it is true in the way a metaphor is true — not in its letter, but in what it makes you notice. It makes you notice that remembering is not retrieval. It is inscription.

Plato gives the image its force by pushing it to its end. [1] The student who has lived long does not add to his stores; [2] The hardening is not preservation — it is crowding. The wax keeps less, not more, the more it has received. That is the first surprise: a full memory is a poor memory.

Modern neuroscience arrives, by experiment, at the same image. [3] The word that carries the whole weight is maintain. The cortex holds the representation by actively maintaining it — the way you hold a note, not the way you keep a jar. The ancient crowding and the modern maintenance are the same admission, separated by two millennia: memory is work. It is not a thing you have; it is a thing you do.

And here the argument turns on itself, which is what arguments do when they are honest. If memory is work, then a good memory is not a full one. The wax that has room, the cortex that can maintain, are the ones that have let go. Forgetting is not the failure of memory; it is part of the making — the release that leaves the tablet soft enough to write on again. The self-education Plato recommends is [4] — the thousand small acts of looking outward, judging by evidence, [5] — by which a mind keeps its wax soft.

The shroud of memory is never finished. We weave it forward by day, and the honest weaver also unweaves — because the argument, argued far enough, discovers that it had the wrong idea all along, and the wrong idea is kept on the record, struck through, not deleted. What remains is not a record but a making. The tablet hardens, the cortex maintains, the thread is pulled loose at night — and what is left is not what we remembered, but the work of remembering. That work is the essay. That work is the self.
