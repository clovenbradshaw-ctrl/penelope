The essay

Memory is not a storehouse; it is a hardening. Plato gives the image: [R@524321]✗ The waxen tablet of the memory which was once capable of receiving 'true thoughts and clear impressions' becomes hard and crowded — [R@522301]✓ there is not room for the accumulations of a long life. The wax was once capable of receiving 'true thoughts and clear impressions'; age crowds it.

What follows is not accumulation but exchange: [R@524321]✗ The student, as years advance, rather makes an exchange of knowledge than adds to his stores. The student trades knowledge for knowledge, and [R@524321]✗ Neither is it easy to give a definite answer to any one who asks how he is to improve.

The remedy is not a method but a thousand small things: [R@524321]✗ For self-education consists in a {ccxi} thousand things, commonplace in themselves, — [R@524321]✗ in adding to what we are by nature something of what we are not, [R@522877]✓ in learning to see ourselves as others see us, [R@524321]✗ in judging, not by opinion, but by the evidence of facts, [R@524321]✗ in any effort of mind which calls forth some latent power.

So the wax hardens, and what saves it is what the wax cannot do alone: the unhistoric acts of looking outward, judging by evidence, calling forth the latent power. Memory is the hardening; the thousand commonplace things are the softening. At the end, the crowded tablet rests.
